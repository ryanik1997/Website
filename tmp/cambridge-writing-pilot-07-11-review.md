### c2-test-07-task-01

School exchange visits are often justified by the opportunities they provide for cultural immersion and language development. Yet in practice, many programmes prioritise convenience over genuine engagement. Students are housed in comfortable hotels rather than with host families, itineraries are packed with tourist attractions rather than meaningful local interactions, and the visiting group rarely has to adapt to local norms. The result is an experience that may be enjoyable but fails to challenge participants or foster real intercultural understanding. If exchange visits are to be ethically defensible, they must be designed to disrupt the visitor's comfort zone, not merely to provide a pleasant holiday abroad.

The most serious ethical problem with school exchange visits is not what happens during the trip but what happens afterwards. Host communities often invest considerable resources—time, hospitality, emotional labour—without any lasting benefit to themselves. The visiting students return home with enriched perspectives, while the hosts are left with little more than a memory. If exchanges are to be truly reciprocal, organisers must consider the long-term impact on the host community. This might involve follow-up projects, financial contributions to local schools, or sustained partnerships that outlast any single visit. Without such commitments, exchange programmes risk being a form of educational tourism that exploits the goodwill of others.

### c2-test-08-task-01

In many cities, neighbourhood transport improvements are concentrated in areas that already have good services. New metro lines, cycle lanes and bus rapid transit systems tend to serve commercial centres and affluent suburbs, while peripheral housing estates and low-income districts continue to rely on infrequent, unreliable buses. This pattern reinforces existing inequalities: residents of well-connected areas gain better access to jobs, education and healthcare, while those in poorly served neighbourhoods face longer journey times and higher transport costs. The result is a mobility gap that entrenches social and economic disadvantage, making it harder for people in less connected areas to improve their circumstances.

Critics of the 'mobility gap' argument claim that transport investment cannot solve deeper structural problems such as housing affordability and labour market segregation. They point out that even when public transport is improved in deprived areas, residents may still lack the skills or opportunities to access better jobs. Moreover, they argue that focusing on transport distracts from more fundamental issues like education, healthcare and social housing. According to this view, transport policy should be guided by efficiency and demand, not by a desire to reduce inequality. Spending limited resources on low-ridership routes in peripheral areas may be wasteful when the same money could improve services where they are most used.

### c2-test-09-task-01

Public libraries have evolved far beyond their traditional role as repositories of books. In many communities, they now function as vital civic hubs, offering free internet access, workspaces, and a range of social programmes. For people who cannot afford private study spaces or reliable broadband, the library is often the only place where they can participate fully in digital society. This expanded role, however, brings new responsibilities. Libraries must balance the demand for quiet study with the need for community events, and they must serve diverse populations with sometimes conflicting needs. The challenge is to remain a welcoming space for everyone while adapting to rapidly changing social expectations. Those who defend the traditional library model argue that this broadening of purpose risks diluting the core mission of promoting literacy and learning. Yet the evidence suggests that libraries that embrace a civic role are more valued and better funded than those that cling to a narrow identity.

The idea that public libraries should become all-purpose community centres is a well-intentioned but dangerous distraction. Libraries were founded on the principle of universal access to knowledge, and that mission is already under threat from budget cuts and digital disruption. Adding social services, such as housing advice or mental health support, stretches already limited resources and blurs the institution's purpose. Staff are trained as librarians, not social workers, and expecting them to handle complex social problems is unrealistic and unfair. Moreover, the push to turn libraries into civic hubs often leads to a reduction in book stock and quiet reading space, alienating the very users who value libraries for their original purpose. If libraries try to be everything to everyone, they risk becoming mediocre at everything. A more responsible approach would be to focus on what libraries do best: providing free access to information and fostering a culture of reading and independent learning.

### c2-test-10-task-01

Community gardens are often celebrated as oases of local food and neighbourly cooperation, yet their governance frequently prioritises the convenience of existing plot-holders over the wider public good. Waiting lists stretch for years, and newcomers—especially those from lower-income backgrounds—are effectively excluded. If we are serious about food justice, we must treat community gardens as public infrastructure, not private allotments. This means reserving a proportion of plots for new applicants, offering reduced fees, and providing tools and seeds free of charge. Convenience for current users should not trump the responsibility to make fresh food accessible to everyone who needs it.

Demanding that community gardens function as free food banks misunderstands their purpose. These spaces thrive because committed volunteers invest time, money and skill in maintaining them. If plots are handed out without expectation of contribution, the gardens will quickly deteriorate. The real responsibility is to cultivate a culture of stewardship—teaching newcomers how to grow food, compost and share harvests sustainably. Long-term viability depends on members who care for the land, not on policies that treat gardens as convenience stores. Access must be balanced with accountability; otherwise, the very resource we seek to protect will collapse under the weight of good intentions.

### c2-test-11-task-01

Local festivals are often celebrated as expressions of community identity, but their organisation increasingly prioritises visitor convenience over long-term civic responsibility. Temporary infrastructure, single-use decorations and excessive waste are accepted as inevitable costs of a successful event. Yet festivals could be designed to strengthen local resilience rather than deplete it. By mandating reusable systems, sourcing materials from within the community, and embedding educational components about sustainability, organisers can transform festivals into models of responsible stewardship. The challenge is not technical but cultural: we must stop treating festivals as exceptions to normal standards and start holding them to the same ethical expectations we apply to everyday life.

Demanding that local festivals become exemplars of sustainability places an unfair burden on volunteer organisers and risks killing the spontaneity that makes these events appealing. Festivals are, by nature, temporary and celebratory; expecting them to solve deep-seated environmental problems is unrealistic. Moreover, the most significant environmental impacts of festivals—travel emissions, energy consumption—are beyond the control of local committees. The real responsibility lies with governments and industries, not with community volunteers who already give their time generously. Insisting on perfect eco-credentials may discourage communities from holding festivals at all, which would be a greater loss to social cohesion than any amount of waste.
